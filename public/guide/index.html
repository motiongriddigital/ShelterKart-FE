A word from the founder

Why I started ShelterKart

For most of my career I worked as a real estate strategist. I advised buyers, sellers, investors, and the people who sat between them. I worked across land, commercial and residential property, in several countries, on deals large and small. Those years taught me a set of principles, and I still hold them.

Look past the brochure. A brochure is written to sell, and it hides more than it reveals. True value takes real scrutiny of the fundamentals.

Deals are made by people. Every transaction has human motivations behind it, and understanding them is often what unlocks a deal that has stalled.

Patience is a financial skill. Impulse buys produce regret. Informed, patient decisions tend to hold their value.

I don’t oversell, overpromise or manufacture urgency. If something doesn’t make sense, I say so, even when it costs me the deal. I value clarity, structure and responsibility far more than volume.

What I noticed, across every segment I worked in, is that the people with the most information about a property are the people selling it. The people with the most at stake, the buyers, have the least. A land buyer, a commercial tenant, a residential purchaser and an investor all face the same gap: the record that determines whether a deal is sound is scattered across registries, filings, court listings and approvals, and no one is paid to read it for them.

Every party in a transaction is paid when it closes. That is not a criticism of anyone. It is simply the structure of the market, and it means that the one party with no stake in the outcome is usually the buyer’s own research, which is often the weakest part of the process.

ShelterKart exists to close that gap. We read the public record on a property and on the company behind it. We show what the record says, what it doesn’t say, and where the sources disagree. Then we turn every gap into a question to put to the people who should answer it, with a clear description of what a good answer looks like.

We will never tell you whether to buy. That decision belongs to you, and you should make it with your own advisers.

PreeyanccFounder, ShelterKart

This guide is general. It is written for any country, so where the rules differ it says “in many markets” and tells you what to ask a local lawyer. It is not legal, tax, financial or investment advice, and it does not tell you whether to buy.

Chapter 1

Before you shortlist

What you are really buying

In brief

  • Decide what the purchase is for before you see a brochure.
  • The price is where the cost begins, not where it ends.
  • Write your must-haves and deal-breakers first.
The friend

You are not buying a flat, a plot or an office. You are buying a number of years of your life in one place, a promise about the future, and a liability you will carry for a long time. Most expensive mistakes are made before the first site visit: in the budget, the timeline and the reason.

The philosopher

Start with one question: what is this purchase for? A home to live in, a property to rent out, land to hold, an asset to sell later. Each has a different test.

  • To live in: judged on the years you will actually use it.
  • To rent out: judged on yield, vacancy and how easily it can be let.
  • To hold: judged on how easily you can exit, and what it costs to wait.
  • To resell: judged on liquidity, and on what the next buyer will want.

Pick one primary purpose and write it down. Most regret comes from buying for one reason and discovering you needed another. Then ask a second question: what would make me regret this in five years?

The mathematician

The price is where the cost begins, not where it ends.

Total cost of owning = price + one-time costs + running costs + cost of money − what you can sell it for.

  • One-time costs: transfer or stamp taxes, registration, legal fees, broker fees, loan processing, furnishing and moving. Depending on the market these add a few percent of the price, or more than ten percent. Ask a local adviser for your market’s figures. Do not accept a round number from a seller.
  • Running costs: maintenance or service charges, property tax, insurance, utilities and repairs.
  • Cost of money: loan interest, plus what the cash you have tied up could have earned elsewhere.

Suppose the price is 100 and the one-time costs are 8. You have spent 108 before you have lived there a day.

Run two stress tests before you go further:

  1. Rates and income. Raise the loan rate by two percentage points and cut your household income by a fifth. Can you carry the payments for a full year? If not, the purchase is too tight.
  2. Waiting. If the home is delayed by twelve months, what do you pay in rent and interest while you wait? Chapter 6 works this out.
The lawyer

At this stage nothing should bind you, but some things do. A booking form or a token payment can be a contract. Before you pay anything, ask whether it is refundable, what happens to it if you change your mind, and exactly what you are signing. In many markets the booking form is the seller’s own draft of the deal. If it matters, have a lawyer read it first.

The consultant

Write three lists before you see a brochure:

  • Must-haves: without these you will not buy.
  • Nice-to-haves: things you would like.
  • Deal-breakers: things that end the conversation.

Sellers sell nice-to-haves, because they are easy to photograph. Your must-haves and deal-breakers are what protect you. When you tour, check what you see against your lists, not against the show home.

The emotional beat

The show home is designed to make you feel something. The lighting, the furniture and the view from the model window are not the home you will receive. It is fine to feel the pull. Name it: “I like how this feels. Is it in my must-haves?” Then take the decision home and sleep on it.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

The report does not choose for you, and it does not judge the project. It checks the project’s public record against the things that most often matter to your lists: the seller, the approvals, the promised dates and the area. It shows where the marketing and the record differ. Where the record cannot answer a question on your list, the report gives you the question to ask.

Questions to put to yourself first

  1. What is this purchase for, in one sentence?
  2. What are my three must-haves and my three deal-breakers?
  3. What is the total cost once every one-time cost is added?
  4. Can I carry the payments if rates rise two points and income falls a fifth?
  5. How old will everyone in my household be on the day I am meant to move in?
  6. What is my plan if the home is twelve months late?
  7. Who gains if I say yes today?
  8. What would make me regret this in five years?

Chapter 2

Who is actually selling to you

In brief

  • The brand is not the entity that signs.
  • Know who signs, who owns the land and who stands behind the promise.
  • Ask for names, numbers and a track record, in writing.
The friend

The name on the brochure is often not the company that signs your contract. Many large developers build each project through a separate company created for that project and owned by a bigger group. The brand is marketing. The contract is with the legal entity. Your rights run against the entity that signs, so that is the one to know.

The lawyer

Identify four parties and keep them apart:

  • The landowner: who holds the title to the land.
  • The developer: who has the right to build and sell on it, by ownership, a joint venture or a development agreement.
  • The project company: the legal entity that signs your agreement and receives your money.
  • The builder: who constructs it.

These can be one company or four. For each, get the legal name, the registration number and the registered address. Then ask which of them gives you each promise: title, completion, quality and the repair of defects. In many markets the agreement and the payment receipts show which entity is the seller. Check that the name on every receipt matches the name on the agreement.

The mathematician

Two simple measures show how much stands behind a promise.

  1. Distance. Count the companies between the brand and the entity that signs. Every layer is a place where the brand’s reputation and the legal obligation can separate.
  2. Whose money it is. Divide the buyer money collected by the capital the company itself put in. If buyers have funded most of the project, buyers are in effect its lenders. That is common, and it is not wrong. But it means the promise is only as strong as the group’s willingness to support it, so ask who stands behind the project company.

Both are questions, not verdicts.

The engineer

Ask who builds, and what they have built. A builder’s finished projects, with their promised and actual completion dates and their defect history, tell you more than a brochure about the next one. Visit a completed project and talk to the people who live there.

The consultant

Ask for these in writing:

  • The legal name and registration number of the selling company, the landowner and the builder.
  • The land title, or the development agreement that gives the right to sell.
  • A list of completed projects, with the promised and the actual completion dates.
  • A schedule of legal cases involving the company and the project, with their status.
  • The account your payments go into, and whether the law in this market requires it to be a protected or designated account.

A good answer arrives within a day, with documents. A weak answer is “our brand is trusted by thousands.”

The philosopher

A reputation is a promise made by a name. A contract is made by an entity. Trust the name if you like. Sign only with the entity, and make sure the entity can keep the promise.

The emotional beat

The salesperson may be warm, capable and honest. That person is still not the party you would take to court. Keep the warmth. Keep the paper.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

  • The registered seller, compared with the name the project is marketed under.
  • The people and addresses the seller’s company shares with other companies in its group, shown as facts and never as conclusions.
  • Completion dates promised and filed on the record.
  • Cases and filings that official records state, each with its link and date.

Where the record is silent, the report says so and gives you the question to ask.

Questions to put to the seller’s team

  1. Which exact legal entity signs my agreement and receives my money?
  2. Who owns the land, and what gives you the right to sell it?
  3. Which company builds, and which projects has it completed?
  4. For your last three completed projects, what were the promised and the actual completion dates?
  5. Which legal cases involve the company or this project, and what is their status?
  6. Into which account do my payments go, and who can withdraw from it?
  7. If the project company cannot finish, who stands behind the promise?

Chapter 3

The approvals

What must exist before a unit is sold

In brief

  • Permission for one phase is not permission for the next.
  • Check that the area sold fits the area approved.
  • Verify every approval on the regulator's own page.
The friend

A beautiful tower can be sold before every permission to build it exists. Approvals are the skeleton of the deal. If one is missing, the building can be stopped, fined or left half-finished, and you are the one holding the payment receipts.

The lawyer

In most markets the stack looks like this, though the names and the order differ:

  • The right to the land: title, or a development right over it.
  • Planning or zoning permission: what may be built, how high and how dense.
  • The building permit or commencement approval, often phase by phase.
  • Safety and utility clearances: fire, environmental, aviation, water and power, depending on the project.
  • Registration with the property regulator, where one exists, with its project number, promised dates and approved area.
  • At the end, the completion or occupancy certificate, without which you may not be allowed to move in or to receive a clean title.

Two rules. Permission for one phase is not permission for the next. And every approval has a date and an expiry, so check both.

The mathematician

Check the arithmetic of area. Suppose you are told the number of homes and the area of each, and the approvals state the total area that may be built.

Homes sold × area per home ≤ approved area × usable share

The usable share is below 100% because corridors, lifts, stairs, walls and shared spaces take part of every building. What counts as “area” varies by market, so ask which definition each number uses.

For example, 100 homes of 70 square metres each is 7,000 of sold area. If the approved total is 6,000, the sold area is larger than the approved area, and the numbers do not reconcile.

That is not proof of wrongdoing. Areas are measured in different ways, and homes can be counted across phases. But it is a question you should have answered before you pay.

The engineer

Read the approved drawings, not the brochure. Check the number of floors, the height, the parking, the fire exits and the open space against what you were shown. Ask who the structural engineer of record is, and where the structural design approval sits. The floors in the brochure and the floors in the approval should match.

The consultant

Ask to see each approval in the original, with its date and validity. Then look it up yourself on the regulator’s public page, so you are comparing the paper you were handed with the official record. Ask what is still pending, and by when. A good answer is a list of documents with dates. A weak answer is “all approvals are in place,” with no papers.

The philosopher

A promise without permission is a hope. Ask for the permission before you invest the hope.

The emotional beat

Asking for papers when everyone is smiling can feel rude. It is not. A good seller expects it and answers readily. A seller who bristles is telling you something.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

  • The project’s registration with the regulator, with its dates and any extensions.
  • Whether the area sold reconciles with the approved area, where both are on the record.
  • The number of floors marketed, against the number approved.
  • Whether the approvals on the record cover the phase you are buying.

Where documents are not public, the report says so and tells you what to ask for.

Questions to put to the seller’s team

  1. Which approvals are in place for the phase I am buying, with their dates and expiry?
  2. What is the project’s registration number with the regulator, and where can I verify it?
  3. What is the total approved area, and how does it compare with the area of the homes on sale?
  4. How many floors are approved, and does that match the brochure?
  5. Which approvals are still pending, and when are they expected?
  6. Who is the structural engineer of record, and where is the design approval?
  7. When will the completion or occupancy certificate be applied for, and by whom?

Chapter 4

The agreement

The only document that counts

In brief

  • If a promise is not in the agreement, treat it as not made.
  • Compare what a delay costs you with what the contract pays.
  • Read it as if the relationship has already gone wrong.
The friend

Everything said in the sales office is a conversation. The agreement is the contract. If a promise is not in it, treat it as not made. Read it slowly, at home, before you pay anything beyond a refundable booking amount.

The lawyer

These clauses most often decide how a purchase goes. The wording and the legal minimums differ by market, so local counsel decides what applies to you.

  • Parties and the property: exactly who, and a description precise enough that nothing can be swapped.
  • Price, and what it includes: and what it leaves out, such as parking, club membership, taxes and connection charges.
  • The payment schedule: tied to dates, or tied to stages of construction? A stage-linked schedule keeps your payments behind the work.
  • The possession date, any grace period, and what counts as “possession.”
  • The seller’s delay: the compensation or interest you receive, and your right to cancel and be refunded.
  • Changes: how far the plans, the area or the specification can change without your consent, and what happens to the price when they do.
  • Your default: what the seller can keep or charge if you pay late, and whether the same rate applies when the seller is late.
  • Title and transfer: when ownership passes to you, and what happens to your claim if the seller fails.
  • Defects: the defect liability period, and who repairs what.
  • Amenities and specifications: listed in a schedule, not in a brochure.
  • Maintenance: who runs the building after handover, what you pay, and when the residents take control.
  • Disputes: the governing law, where disputes are heard, and whether arbitration is required.

In many markets the law sets minimum protections or standard forms for sale agreements. Ask a local lawyer which apply.

The mathematician

Work out what a delay costs you each month.

Money you have paid × your cost of money ÷ 12 + rent you are still paying

Compare that with what the contract gives you for each month of delay. If the contract pays less than your cost, the gap is yours.

Suppose you have paid 50 units, your cost of money is 9% a year, and you still pay rent of 0.4 units a month. Your monthly cost is 50 × 0.09 ÷ 12 = 0.375, plus 0.4, which is 0.775. If the contract pays 0.2 a month, you lose 0.575 a month, or 6.9 over a year of delay.

Also check the two directions. If you pay 12% a year when you are late and the seller pays you 6% when it is late, the contract is lopsided.

The engineer

The specification schedule should name materials and grades, not adjectives. “Premium tiles” is an adjective. A brand, a thickness and a grade is a specification. Watch for “or equivalent,” and ask who decides what is equivalent. Ask what you can see before you commit: a sample, a finished home, a test report. Ask for the structure type, the floor-to-floor height and the waterproofing method.

The consultant

These phrases are not accusations. They show where the seller keeps discretion, so question each one:

  • “Subject to approvals.”
  • “At the sole discretion of the developer.”
  • “As may be decided from time to time.”
  • “Approximately,” next to an area.
  • A variation allowance with no limit.
  • A penalty that applies only to you.
  • A schedule left blank, or “to be provided later.”

Practical rules: read every page. Never sign blank pages, or pages with blanks. Get any promise made in the sales office written into the agreement. Ask for changes in writing. Keep a signed copy of everything. Have your own lawyer read it before a token payment becomes a contract.

A good answer to a request for a change is “Yes, we can add that clause,” or a clear reason for no. A weak answer is “That is our standard. Nobody changes it.”

The philosopher

A contract is a plan for the day goodwill runs out. Read it as if the relationship has already gone wrong, because that is the only day it will be used.

The emotional beat

Signing day brings pressure: the queue of other buyers, the deadline, the offer that ends today. If an agreement is good for you, it will still be good tomorrow. Take the pages home.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

ShelterKart usually cannot see your agreement, because it is not public. Where the record shows the standard form or the key terms a regulator requires, the report shows them. It also lists the clauses an agreement for your market should address, so you can compare them with the draft you were given. And it checks the dates in the record against the dates in the brochure.

Questions to put to the seller’s team

  1. Can I take the full agreement home, with every schedule, before I pay more than a refundable booking amount?
  2. Is the payment schedule tied to dates, or to stages of construction?
  3. What is the possession date, and what exactly counts as possession?
  4. What do I receive for each month of delay, and can I cancel and be refunded?
  5. Is the delay compensation at the same rate I pay if I am late?
  6. How far can the plans, the area or the specification change without my consent?
  7. Where are the amenities and specifications listed, by brand and grade?
  8. Which promises made in the sales office are written into the agreement?

Chapter 5

The money

Price, payment plan and everything on top

In brief

  • Compare payment plans by what they cost today.
  • Compare homes by price per unit of carpet area.
  • Pay only what the agreement states, into the account it names.
The friend

Two buyers can pay for the same home and end up with different bills. The number in the brochure is the start of the conversation. The number that matters is what leaves your account, when it leaves, and what you receive for it.

The mathematician

A payment plan has a price in time as well as in money. Money paid later is worth less to you than money paid now, because you could use it in the meantime. To compare plans fairly, bring every payment back to today’s value:

Value today of a plan = payment at month 0 + payment at month t ÷ (1 + r)t + … for every payment

Here r is your own yearly cost of money and t is the time in years.

Suppose Plan A is 100 paid today. Plan B is 20 today, 20 in twelve months and 60 in three years. At r = 8%:

20 + 20 ÷ 1.08 + 60 ÷ 1.08³ = 20 + 18.52 + 47.63 = 86.15

Plan B is worth about 14 less to you than Plan A. Now check the trap. If Plan B carries a price 15% higher, its value today is 86.15 × 1.15 = 99.07, which is the same as Plan A. A deferred plan is only a saving if the price is not raised to pay for it, and only if the home arrives on time.

Compare areas the same way. Offer A is 900 for 60 of carpet area, which is 15 per unit. Offer B is 1,000 for 80 of “saleable” area. Ask B for its carpet area. If it is 55, the price is 18.2 per unit. The bigger number on the brochure was the smaller home. Chapter 7 explains the area terms.

The lawyer

Pay only what the agreement states, to the account it names, by a traceable method, and keep a receipt for every payment in the name of the entity that signed. Check these points:

  • The account. In many markets the law requires buyer money to go into a designated or protected account. Ask whether that applies here and which account it is.
  • The booking amount. Is it refundable, and is it credited against the price?
  • Taxes and charges. Which taxes apply to the purchase, who collects them and who pays them?
  • Payments outside the contract. Money that does not appear in the agreement is not protected by it. If the project fails, you hold no receipt and no claim. In many markets an undeclared payment is also unlawful for the buyer as well as the seller. A discount for paying part outside the books saves less than it risks.
The engineer

Match the plan to the work. A stage-linked plan asks for a payment when a defined stage is complete, such as the foundation, a slab or the roof. Ask who certifies each stage, and ask to see the certificate before you pay. Chapter 6 shows how to compare what you have paid with what has been built.

The consultant

Ask for the all-in cost on one page, with every line:

  • Base price.
  • Parking, storage and any premium for floor, view or corner.
  • Club, amenity and utility connection charges.
  • Maintenance deposits and sinking or reserve fund contributions.
  • Transfer, stamp and registration taxes, and legal fees.
  • Any brokerage you are being asked to pay.

If a line is “to be advised,” ask for the range in writing.

The philosopher

Price is what you pay. Value is what you receive. Most of the difference between them lives in details that nobody wants to discuss.

The emotional beat

“This offer ends today” is built to shorten your thinking. A good offer will survive a week. If it does not, you have saved yourself money.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

Where a market publishes recorded sale prices, the report puts them next to the price you were quoted. It shows the area definitions the project’s registered documents use, and the charges those documents name. Where the record cannot show a figure, the report gives you the question to ask.

Questions to put to the seller’s team

  1. What is the all-in cost on one page, with every charge and every tax?
  2. Is the payment plan tied to dates, or to certified stages of construction?
  3. Is the price on the deferred plan the same as on the up-front plan?
  4. Which account do I pay into, and in whose name?
  5. Is the booking amount refundable, and is it credited to the price?
  6. What is the carpet area, measured inside the walls, and how is it measured?
  7. Which charges will I pay after handover, and how much are they each year?
  8. Can you confirm in writing that every payment I make will appear in the agreement and in a receipt?

Chapter 6

Time

The possession date and what it does to everything else

In brief

  • Treat the promised date as the earliest, not the likeliest.
  • Correct it by the seller's own record.
  • Do not let what you pay run ahead of what is built.
The friend

The date in the brochure is the most important number in it and the least tested. A building is a long chain of dependent steps, and each link can slip. Treat the promised date as the earliest the home could arrive, not the day it will.

The philosopher

Time is the one asset nobody can buy back. A late home costs you months of your life as well as money.

The mathematician

Psychologists call the habit of underestimating how long a project will take the planning fallacy. It affects buyers, sellers and builders alike, and it persists even when everyone knows that similar projects ran late. The forecaster’s remedy is to look at how comparable projects actually performed and to correct the promise by that record:

Expected remaining time = promised remaining time × (actual time ÷ promised time on the seller’s past projects)

Suppose 24 months remain on the promise, and the seller’s past projects took on average 1.4 times as long as promised. Expect about 34 months. Plan your life around 34, not 24.

Then price the wait. Using the monthly cost from Chapter 4 (0.775 a month in that example), ten extra months cost 7.75 before any compensation.

Finally, compare what you have paid with what has been built:

Payment ratio = money paid ÷ price. Progress ratio = certified work complete ÷ total work.

If the payment ratio is higher than the progress ratio, you are lending to the project. If you have paid 40% and the certified work is 25% complete, you have financed 15 points of construction in advance. Ask for a plan in which payments follow certified progress.

The engineer

Ask for the stage-wise construction schedule and the certified progress, signed by the independent architect or engineer. Visit at intervals, take dated photographs and compare them. A site in motion shows people, materials and equipment. A site that is quiet for weeks is a question. Ask what sits on the critical path: approvals, financing, materials, the monsoon or winter season.

The lawyer

Four points decide what a delay means:

  • What counts as possession. Keys, the occupancy certificate, or something earlier?
  • The grace period, and whether it is added to the promised date.
  • Extensions. In many markets a regulator or the contract can extend the date. Ask for every extension granted to date and the reasons given.
  • Force majeure. Which events excuse a delay, and what notice must the seller give?

Then check your own rights: the point at which you may cancel, the refund you receive and whether interest is added, and the time limit for giving notice.

The consultant

Ask for the promise in a form you can hold: a month and year in the agreement, not “approximately.” Ask for the promised and actual dates of the last three completed projects. Ask what would delay this one. A good answer names specific risks and who owns them. A weak answer is “we always deliver.”

The emotional beat

A late home disrupts a family’s plan: a school year, a lease ending, a promise to parents. Write down where you will live if the date is twelve months later, and what that costs. If you cannot answer, the purchase is too tight.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

  • The promised completion date against the dates filed on the record, and any extensions.
  • The seller’s earlier projects: promised and filed dates, where records exist.
  • Construction progress reported to a regulator, where it is filed.

Where the record is silent, the report says so and gives you the question to ask.

Questions to put to the seller’s team

  1. What is the possession date, as a month and year in the agreement?
  2. What counts as possession, and is the grace period added to that date?
  3. Which extensions have been granted so far, and for what reasons?
  4. For your last three completed projects, what were the promised and the actual completion dates?
  5. What certified percentage of the work is complete, and who certified it?
  6. Which events would delay this project, and what is the plan for each?
  7. If the date passes by more than a stated limit, can I cancel and receive a refund with interest?
  8. What is your definition of force majeure, and what notice must I receive?

Chapter 7

The build

Area, specification and the site visit

In brief

  • Measure by carpet area, not by the largest number.
  • Check structure, waterproofing and services on evidence.
  • Stand in a finished unit, and bring someone independent.
The friend

You are buying a building that will stand for decades, not a brochure. Quality is invisible until the first heavy rain, the first cold winter or the first year of use. That is why it has to be checked on paper and on site before you pay.

The mathematician

Three area terms cause most confusion. Markets use different names for them, so ask for the definition each time:

  • Carpet or net internal area: the floor inside your walls, where you can stand and place furniture.
  • Built-up area: the carpet area plus the thickness of the walls, balconies and similar spaces.
  • Saleable or super built-up area: the built-up area plus your share of the common spaces, such as lobbies, stairs, lifts and the plant rooms.

The gap between the first and the last is the loading factor:

Loading factor = (saleable area − carpet area) ÷ carpet area

Suppose the carpet area is 60 and the saleable area is 90. The loading factor is 50%. At a price of 10 per saleable unit, you pay 900 in all, and 300 of it is for space outside your walls. Compare homes by price per unit of carpet area, never by the larger number in the brochure.

The International Property Measurement Standards exist so that floor areas can be compared across markets. Many markets still use their own definitions, so measure what you are given.

The engineer

Ask for evidence on each of these, and have an independent professional read it with you:

  • Foundations and structure: the soil investigation, the foundation type, the structural system, the design code and the engineer of record. Where earthquakes, strong winds or floods are a risk, ask how the design addresses them.
  • Fire safety: the number and width of stairs, the exits, and the fire-fighting systems.
  • Waterproofing: bathrooms, terraces, basements and the facade. Water damage is among the most common defects.
  • Light and air: the orientation of the windows and the direction of the afternoon sun, cross-ventilation, and the clear ceiling height inside a room, which is lower than the floor-to-floor height.
  • Services: the source and storage of water, sewage treatment, electrical capacity and backup, and whether the lifts are enough for the number of residents.
  • Parking and access: the ratio of spaces to homes, and step-free access.
  • Energy: where an energy rating exists for new buildings, ask for it.

The site visit.

  1. Stand in a finished, empty unit of the same type, not in the furnished show home.
  2. Go at two different times of day, and in the rain if you can.
  3. Look at the neighbours: the plots around the site, and what may be built on them.
  4. Visit a completed project by the same builder that is three to five years old. Look for cracks, stains, seepage and how the common areas are kept.
  5. Hire an independent inspector or surveyor, who is not paid by the seller, before your final payment and again at handover.
The lawyer

The specification schedule in the agreement is a contract. The brochure is not. Check for “or equivalent” and for rights to substitute materials. Ask what warranties cover the structure, the waterproofing and the fittings, who gives them, and for how long.

The consultant

Ask for the specification by brand, grade and thickness. Ask for a sample of each major finish. Ask to see the structural design approval. A good answer is a document, a sample or a visit. A weak answer is “it is premium quality.”

The philosopher

A home is the building you will live in, not the building you were shown.

The emotional beat

A show home is furnished, lit and staged to make you feel something. Enjoy it, and then ask to stand in an empty, finished unit.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

  • The number of floors marketed, against the number approved.
  • The areas stated, against the areas in the registered documents.
  • Which approvals and specifications appear on the record.

Where an item is not on the record, the report turns it into a question for the seller’s team.

Questions to put to the seller’s team

  1. How are the carpet, built-up and saleable areas of my home measured, and what is the carpet area?
  2. What is the loading factor?
  3. What is the clear ceiling height inside the rooms?
  4. Who is the structural engineer of record, and what is the design code?
  5. What waterproofing method is used on bathrooms, terraces and basements, and what warranty applies?
  6. Where is the specification listed, by brand, grade and thickness?
  7. Can I stand in a finished, empty unit and visit a completed project of yours?
  8. May an independent inspector of my choice inspect my home before the final payment?

Chapter 8

The life you will actually live

Amenities, neighbourhood and your family's timeline

In brief

  • Run your household's ages forward to the day you move in.
  • Value what exists today, not what is planned.
  • Price the running cost of every amenity.
The friend

Amenities sell homes. The brochure shows a swimming pool full of laughing children, a lawn, a clubhouse. But you will not move into the photograph. You will move into the life you have on the day the keys arrive, and that life may not contain the people in the picture.

The mathematician

Start with the ages of everyone in your household on the day you will actually move in.

Age at possession = age today + years until possession

Use the expected date from Chapter 6, not the promised one. A child of 10 and a promise of five years gives 15. If the realistic wait is six years, it gives 16.

Now take an amenity with an age band, such as a play area:

Usable years = the upper age of the equipment − age at possession, never less than zero

If the equipment is rated up to age 12, the usable years for that child are 12 − 15, which is zero. The play area is still a good feature. It is simply no longer a feature for that child.

Put a rough value on any amenity this way:

Value to you ≈ usable years × how often you will use it × what the same use costs elsewhere × the chance it arrives on time

The last term matters. An amenity in a late phase may arrive after the years in which you would have used it.

The same sums apply to the people at the other end of life. A lift, step-free access and a shaded walking path matter more with every year, so run the ages of older family members forward as well.

Then price the running cost. Residents pay for amenities through service charges, whether or not they use them. If a club costs 600,000 a year to run and 300 homes share it, each home pays 2,000 a year.

And price your commute:

Hours a year = round-trip hours a day × working days a year

Two hours a day over 250 days is 500 hours a year, about twenty full days. Set that against any saving in price.

The engineer

Ask which phase delivers each amenity, and whether it appears in the approved plans. An amenity on a podium, a roof or the last block depends on the construction of that structure. Ask who will maintain it and how the cost will be shared.

The lawyer

An amenity counts if it is named in the agreement’s schedule. Check whether the use of an amenity is included in the price or sold as a separate membership, who owns the common areas, and what happens if an amenity is not delivered or is changed.

The consultant

Value what exists today. A planned metro line, school, hospital or road has its own timeline, and the same planning fallacy applies to it. Check the official plans for the land around the project: what could be built next door, what could block a view or add noise or traffic.

Then do this exercise. For each person in your household, write their age at possession, five years later and ten years later. Beside each, list the five amenities they would actually use. Compare your lists with the five the brochure shows.

Also think about resale. A home that suits only one stage of life narrows the pool of people who will want to buy it from you.

The philosopher

Value is a relationship between a thing and a life. The same pool is worth different things to a five-year-old and a fifteen-year-old.

The emotional beat

Do not buy for the child you imagine. Buy for the family you will be. And resist the guilt that says a good parent would never turn down the pool.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

  • Which amenities appear in the project’s registered documents, and which phase each belongs to.
  • Infrastructure claims, such as nearby transport or schools, against the official plans, where those are on the record.

Where the record cannot show an amenity or its phase, the report turns it into a question for the seller’s team.

Questions to put to the seller’s team

  1. Which amenities are listed in the agreement, and in which phase is each delivered?
  2. What age range is the play equipment designed for?
  3. What will each amenity cost residents each year, and who will run it?
  4. If an amenity is late or changed, what do I receive?
  5. Which amenities are included in the price, and which are paid memberships?
  6. What has been approved for the land and roads around the project?
  7. Which planned infrastructure has an approved date, and which is only proposed?
  8. When do residents take over the management of the common areas?

Chapter 9

Financing, tax and currency

In brief

  • Stress-test the loan at two points higher.
  • Price tax across buying, holding and selling.
  • Treat a currency move as part of the price.
The friend

Most buyers borrow. The loan is often the largest commitment you will make, and it outlasts the excitement of the purchase by twenty years. Treat it as a decision of its own, not a detail of the purchase.

The mathematician

A loan with equal monthly payments costs:

Payment = L × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1)

Here L is the amount borrowed, r is the monthly interest rate (the yearly rate ÷ 12) and n is the number of months.

Borrow 100 for 20 years at 8% a year. The payment is 0.836 a month for each 100 borrowed. Over 240 months you repay about 200.7, so the interest is about 100.7. You repay roughly twice what you borrowed.

Now raise the rate to 10%. The payment becomes 0.965 a month, and the total repaid rises to about 231.6. A two-point rise in the rate lifts the monthly payment by about 15%. This is the stress test from Chapter 1.

For a home that is still being built, lenders often pay the seller in stages as work is certified. You pay interest on what has been drawn so far, and you may also still pay rent. That is the monthly cost of waiting from Chapter 4, and it is why a late home costs more than it first appears.

If the price is in a currency other than the one you earn, the exchange rate is part of the price.

Cost in your currency = price ÷ the rate at the time you pay

If your currency falls 10% against the currency of the price, each payment of 100 costs you 100 ÷ 0.9 = 111, about 11% more. The spread matters too. Compare the rate a bank or transfer provider gives you with the mid-market rate:

Cost of conversion = (mid-market rate − the rate you receive) ÷ mid-market rate

Payments spread over years average the rate, but they also carry its risk. Ask a bank about fixing the rate in advance for known payments.

The lawyer

Read the loan agreement as carefully as the sale agreement:

  • Fixed or floating rate, and how often a floating rate resets.
  • Prepayment charges if you repay early or refinance.
  • What happens if the project is delayed: do you keep paying interest, and on what amount?
  • The lender’s claim on the property, and what happens if the seller fails.
  • Insurance the lender requires, and who pays for it.
  • Source-of-funds checks. Expect to document where your deposit came from. Anti-money-laundering rules apply in most markets.

In some markets lenders keep lists of projects they have approved. An approval is a useful signal and not a guarantee of quality or delivery.

The consultant

Tax belongs in the first sum, not the last. Ask a local tax adviser to price the whole cycle:

  • Buying: transfer, stamp or registration taxes, and in some markets sales tax or VAT on new buildings.
  • Holding: annual property taxes, and tax on any rent you receive.
  • Selling: tax on the gain, and whether the length of time you hold the property or your residency changes it.

Do not count on a tax deduction for your loan until an adviser has confirmed that it applies to you.

The philosopher

Debt is a promise your present self makes on behalf of your future self. Make sure your future self was consulted.

The emotional beat

A bank’s approval feels like a verdict, but it is not one. Lenders approve what you can repay under their rules, not what you can comfortably live with. The number you are comfortable with is usually lower.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

Financing is outside what a public-record report can check, and the report does not tell you whether to borrow. It helps in one way: it shows the seller’s legal identity and the dates on the record, which your lender will also test.

Questions to put to the lender and the seller

  1. Is the rate fixed or floating, and when does it reset?
  2. What are the prepayment charges?
  3. If the home is delayed, what do I pay each month, and on what amount?
  4. Has the lender approved this project, and does that approval cover my unit?
  5. What are the total taxes and charges for buying, holding and selling?
  6. If my currency falls 10%, what does the next payment cost me?
  7. Can the rate for known future payments be fixed in advance?
  8. Which documents will I need to show where my money came from?

Chapter 10

Buying from abroad

In brief

  • Hire your own local lawyer.
  • Limit any power of attorney.
  • Pay only to an account you have verified yourself.
The friend

Distance multiplies every risk in this book. You cannot see the site, you may not read the language of the contract, and you cannot walk into the registry. What you do have is time, and the freedom to refuse haste. Use both.

The lawyer

Five steps carry most of the protection.

  1. Hire your own local lawyer. Do not use one recommended by the seller, or by an agent who is paid when the sale completes. Confirm that the lawyer is licensed in that country. Ask them to check the seller, the title and the approvals, to read the agreement, and to handle registration and tax advice.
  2. Be careful with a power of attorney. It gives another person legal authority over your purchase. A broad one can allow them to sell or mortgage your property. Limit it by scope, purpose and duration, name someone you trust, and ask your lawyer how it can be revoked.
  3. Check the foreign-ownership rules before you fall in love. Some countries restrict foreign buyers by area, property type or quota, allow leasehold only, or require approvals or extra taxes.
  4. Check the rules on money. Find out what is required to bring funds in, what proof of source is needed, and what is required to take the proceeds of a sale out of the country.
  5. Check the paperwork and succession. Documents signed abroad often need notarising, an apostille or consular legalisation, and a certified translation. Local inheritance law may decide who receives the property, and it may differ from the law at home.
The mathematician

Count the whole cost across borders:

All-in cost = price converted at the rate you actually receive + transfer fees + taxes and legal fees + the cost of visits

If you plan to rent the property out, measure its real return:

Net yield = (yearly rent − management fee − vacancy loss − running costs − tax) ÷ all-in price

Suppose the all-in price is 100, the yearly rent is 6, the management fee is 0.6, one month of vacancy costs 0.5, running costs are 1 and tax is 0.8. The net yield is (6 − 0.6 − 0.5 − 1 − 0.8) ÷ 100, which is 3.1%. The headline yield of 6% was nearly twice the real one.

The engineer

You need eyes on the site that are not paid by the seller. Hire an independent inspector for site visits and dated photographs. Ask for a live video walk-through. Compare what you see with the certified schedule from Chapter 6.

The consultant

Seven rules protect your money:

  1. Pay only to the account named in the agreement, in the name of the selling entity.
  2. Verify the account details by calling a number you found yourself, not one in an email or message.
  3. Treat any change of bank details as a warning, and confirm it by phone.
  4. Check any agent’s licence on the regulator’s public register.
  5. Never pay because “the price rises tomorrow.”
  6. Keep every instruction and promise in writing.
  7. Do not rely on a “friend of the developer” who offers a better deal.
The philosopher

Distance is a cost. Count it. Do not simply endure it.

The emotional beat

For many buyers abroad, a purchase is also about home: memory, belonging, family. That is a good reason to buy, and a risky one. When the heart is already there, the head has to stay at the table. Ask someone you trust, who does not share your longing, to read the file.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

The report identifies the legal seller and its registration number from the record, and shows the dates and approvals that official sources state. It puts that record on your screen, in English, wherever you are. It does not replace a local lawyer or a site inspection, and it says so.

Questions to put to your local lawyer and the seller

  1. Are there restrictions on foreign buyers for this type of property?
  2. What is required to bring money in, and to take sale proceeds out?
  3. Do I need a power of attorney, and how can its scope and duration be limited?
  4. Which documents must be notarised or legalised, and translated?
  5. What taxes apply to me as a non-resident, on the purchase, the rent and a later sale?
  6. Who inherits the property under local law if something happens to me?
  7. Can an independent inspector of my choice visit the site and send dated photographs?
  8. Which account do I pay into, and how can I verify it independently?

Chapter 11

When something goes wrong

Delay, defects and disputes

In brief

  • Document first, argue second.
  • Climb the ladder one rung at a time.
  • Estimate what a dispute is worth before you start one.
The friend

Most purchases end well. Plan for the ones that do not, because the buyers who come through best are the ones who prepared before they needed to.

The lawyer

Disputes move up a ladder. Start on the lowest rung that works.

  1. A written notice to the seller: the facts, what you want, and a date for the reply.
  2. The seller’s complaint channel, if the agreement names one.
  3. The regulator’s complaint process, where the market has a property regulator.
  4. Mediation or arbitration, if the agreement or the law provides for it.
  5. Court.

Three cautions apply in every market. Claims have time limits, which run from particular events, so act early. Do not stop paying without legal advice, because the agreement may let the seller cancel or charge you for a late payment. And keep records of everything:

  • The signed agreement with every schedule.
  • Every receipt and bank statement.
  • Emails and messages.
  • Dated photographs and your site-visit notes.
  • A note of each call: date, who, and what was said.

Use writing wherever you can. Confirm any call by email, and send formal notices by a method that proves delivery. Other buyers in the same project may share your problem. Where the law allows, acting together can share the cost.

The mathematician

Before you start a dispute, estimate what it is worth:

Expected net outcome = (chance of success × amount recovered) − (fees and other costs) − the value of your time

Suppose the claim is 50, the chance of success is 60%, costs are 8 and your time is worth 2. The expected net outcome is 0.6 × 50 − 8 − 2 = 20. If the chance falls to 30%, it is 0.3 × 50 − 10 = 5.

Use the same sum to judge an offer. A settlement of 22 today beats an expected 20 in three years, because money later is worth less than money now (Chapter 5). Ask your lawyer for an honest range for the chance of success, and treat a confident single number with caution.

The engineer

For defects, document first and argue second. Take dated photographs, ask an independent inspector for a written report, and notify the seller in writing within the defect liability period. Ask for a repair schedule with dates. At handover, do not accept possession “in full satisfaction” while snags remain. List them in the handover record.

The consultant

Short, specific messages work best. Three templates:

  • “The agreement states possession by [date]. Please confirm in writing the revised date, the reason, and the compensation due under clause [number].”
  • “Please confirm receipt of this notice by [date].”
  • “I noted the following defects on [date]. Please send a repair schedule by [date].”

A good answer is dated, specific and in writing. A weak answer is a promise by phone.

The philosopher

Conflict is expensive and clarity is cheap. Most disputes cost less the earlier they are named.

The emotional beat

People wait too long. They hope, they feel embarrassed to complain, or they fear making things worse. The first written question is the cheapest step you will ever take. Talk to the other buyers. Almost no one is alone in this.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

The report lists orders, complaints and proceedings that official records state about the seller or the project, each with its source and date, so that you start from a known position. Where the sources we could reach show none, the report says exactly that. It does not say that none exist.

Questions to put to the seller’s team before you sign

  1. Which law governs the agreement, and where are disputes heard?
  2. Is arbitration required, and who pays for it?
  3. Is there a regulator complaint route for this project?
  4. What is your complaint process, and what are its time limits?
  5. How are defects reported during the defect liability period, and how quickly are they repaired?
  6. Who pays costs if I win, and if I lose?
  7. How are decisions that affect all buyers taken, and how can buyers act together?
  8. Which proceedings involve the company or this project at present?

Chapter 12

Possession and the first year

In brief

  • The keys are not the title.
  • Reconcile the area and the money.
  • Snag every room, and keep the list.
The friend

The keys feel like the end. They are the point at which your leverage is smallest and your paperwork is least complete. Handover is a process with a checklist, and the first year is when hidden defects appear.

The lawyer

Before you accept possession, ask for these and keep every document:

  • The completion or occupancy certificate, under whatever name your market uses. Ask your lawyer what must exist before you may move in.
  • The final measurement, compared with the area in the agreement.
  • A signed snag list, with a date for each repair.
  • The start date of the defect liability period, in writing.
  • Warranty documents, manuals and the as-built drawings, which show where the pipes and cables run.
  • Meter readings for electricity, water and gas, and the transfer of utilities to your name.
  • Final payments and deposits, exactly as the agreement states them, each with a receipt.
  • Title. The keys are not ownership. Check that the transfer is registered in your name, collect the originals, and have your lawyer confirm there is no charge or claim against the property.
  • The owners’ association: how and when it is formed, when the builder hands over control, who holds the reserve fund, and where the accounts are.

Then arrange insurance, register for property tax and take photographs of every room before the furniture arrives.

The mathematician

Reconcile two things.

Area. Compare the area delivered with the area sold:

Adjustment = (area sold − area delivered) × price per unit of area

Suppose 90 was sold, 87 is delivered and the price is 10 per unit. The shortfall is 3 and the adjustment is 30. Check the tolerance the agreement allows before you claim.

Money. Add up every receipt and check that the total equals what the agreement says you should have paid by now.

Then run your first-year budget:

Year-one cost = loan payments + service charges + property tax + insurance + utilities + repairs

Compare it with the plan you made in Chapter 1, and note every line that differs.

The engineer

Snag the home room by room, with an independent inspector if you can:

  • Walls and ceilings: cracks, damp patches and stains.
  • Doors and windows: they open, close and lock, with even gaps.
  • Electrical: every socket and switch, and the circuit breakers.
  • Plumbing: run every tap, flush every toilet, check pressure and drainage, and look under sinks for leaks.
  • Bathrooms and balconies: ask the inspector to run a water test, and look for slope towards the drains.
  • Floors: tap tiles for hollow sounds, and check that they are level.
  • Ventilation and finishes: extractor fans, paint and joints.

Photograph every defect, with the date.

The consultant

Run the first year on a schedule:

  • Week 1: the snag list, signed.
  • Month 1: check that every repair was done.
  • Month 3: check for damp after the first heavy rain or cold spell.
  • Two months before the defect period ends: a full inspection, and a written list sent to the seller.
  • Throughout: join the owners’ association and read its accounts.
The philosopher

The purchase ends when the paperwork is clean, not when the keys arrive.

The emotional beat

The moment of the keys is joyful, and that is when people sign away their rights. Do not sign a blanket “accepted in full satisfaction” form while snags remain. Enjoy the day, and keep the list.

What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

  • The completion or occupancy certificate, where the record shows its status.
  • The completion date filed on the record, against the date of handover.
  • Orders or proceedings on the record that bear on the project around handover.

Where the record is silent, the report says so and gives you the question to ask.

Questions to put to the seller’s team at handover

  1. Has the completion or occupancy certificate been issued, and may I see it?
  2. What is the final measured area, and how does it compare with the agreement?
  3. Where is the signed snag list, with the repair dates?
  4. When does the defect liability period start and end?
  5. Where are the warranties, manuals and as-built drawings?
  6. When will the title be registered in my name, and who pays the charges?
  7. When does the owners’ association take control of the common areas, and what is in the reserve fund?
  8. Which dues are payable at handover, and are they all in the agreement?

Appendix B

Words you will hear

Markets use different names for the same things, so ask for the local definition each time.

  • Apostille: a certificate that authenticates a public document so that another country can recognise it, in countries that accept it.
  • As-built drawings: drawings of the building as it was actually built, showing where pipes and cables run.
  • Booking or token amount: money paid to reserve a home before the agreement is signed. Whether it is refundable depends on its terms.
  • Built-up area: the carpet area plus the thickness of the walls, balconies and similar spaces.
  • Carpet or net internal area: the floor inside your walls.
  • Charge or lien: a legal claim over a property, often held by a lender, that must be cleared for the owner to have a clean title.
  • Completion or occupancy certificate: a document, under various names, issued by the authority to confirm that a building was completed in line with its approvals and may be occupied.
  • Defect liability period: the time after handover during which the seller must repair defects.
  • Designated or escrow account: an account that holds buyer money for a project and releases it against stated conditions.
  • Encumbrance: a claim or burden on a property, such as a mortgage.
  • Force majeure: events beyond a party’s control that the agreement says excuse a delay.
  • Freehold and leasehold: freehold ownership has no fixed end. Leasehold is the right to use the property for a fixed period.
  • Grace period: extra time after the promised date during which the seller is not treated as late.
  • Handover: the point at which the seller gives you possession.
  • Loading factor: the share of the saleable area that lies outside your walls (Chapter 7).
  • Loan-to-value: the loan as a percentage of the price or value of the property.
  • Owners’ association: the body of owners, known by several names, that runs the common areas after handover.
  • Planning fallacy: the habit of underestimating how long a project will take (Chapter 6).
  • Possession: usually the date you receive the home to use. The agreement defines it.
  • Power of attorney: a document that authorises another person to act for you.
  • Property regulator: a public body that oversees property sales and developers in some markets, such as RERA in India.
  • Reference-class forecast: an estimate based on how comparable past projects actually performed.
  • Saleable or super built-up area: the built-up area plus your share of the common spaces.
  • Service or maintenance charge: what owners pay to run and maintain the building.
  • Sinking or reserve fund: money collected from owners for major future repairs.
  • Snag or snagging: a defect noted at handover and listed for repair.
  • Stage-linked and time-linked plans: in a stage-linked plan you pay when a defined stage of construction is complete. In a time-linked plan you pay on set dates, whatever has been built.
  • Strata: ownership of a unit in a building, together with a share of the common property.
  • Title: the legal right of ownership. A clean title has no competing claims.
C

Appendix C

The buyer's risk register

Professionals manage a large purchase with a risk register. This one is adapted from the process in ISO 31000, the international risk-management standard. It takes an hour to complete and it changes how you decide.

The five steps

  1. Identify. List what could go wrong, using the chapters of this guide.
  2. Analyse. For each risk, rate how likely it is (1 to 5) and how bad it would be (1 to 5).
  3. Evaluate. Multiply the two ratings. As a rule of thumb, a rating of 15 or more needs an action before you pay anything non-refundable. A rating of 8 to 14 needs an action or a decision to accept the risk knowingly. Below 8, note it and watch it.
  4. Treat. Reduce the risk (ask, verify, negotiate), transfer it (insurance, a contract clause), avoid it (do not buy), or accept it knowingly.
  5. Monitor. Review the register at every payment, and whenever something changes.

An example. The ratings below are illustrations. Yours will differ.
